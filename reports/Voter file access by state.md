# Sixteen jurisdictions open voter files to locators

A private company can lawfully obtain and use the statewide voter file for an internal locate-and-link database in **16 of 51 jurisdictions**. In **10 there is no use restriction at all**: Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, the District of Columbia, Florida, Michigan, Mississippi, North Carolina, Ohio and Wisconsin. **Six more allow it with conditions**: Idaho, Indiana (county copies only), New Jersey, Rhode Island, South Carolina and Texas. Each of these six bans a specific act, such as soliciting, mailing, advertising or transferring the data, and does not ban commercial purposes in general. **The other 35 prohibit the use.** They do it in one of three ways: a purpose test that allows only election or political use, a flat ban on "commercial" use, or a gate that keeps private companies from getting the file at all. Penalties run from Connecticut's civil fine of up to $2,000 per offense to felonies in Arizona and Illinois. Two things matter most for planning. First, the open states include Florida, North Carolina, Ohio, Michigan and Texas, which are large states, and Florida and Texas files carry full dates of birth. Second, the rules have tightened over 2025–2026: Connecticut, Alabama, Oklahoma and Utah all closed or narrowed access, and the Connecticut and Alabama bans also cover data a company already holds. Where the file is closed, the substitutes are a patchwork, and each carries its own use limits: occupational-license files, unclaimed-property owner files, court bulk extracts, and permissible-purpose commercial products such as credit header. No single substitute replaces a voter file. Every verdict below is legal research for internal planning, not legal advice. Twenty-eight verdicts rest on primary statute or agency text. Twenty-three rest on secondary summaries and should be confirmed before any acquisition.

## The wording of the restriction decides the outcome

Across the 51 jurisdictions, the decisive question is not whether a state calls its voter list "public." It is the exact form of the use restriction. **Act-based bans** prohibit a defined act: soliciting, mailing or delivering an offer, advertising a product, or transferring the list. An internal database that never contacts anyone sits outside them. **Purpose-based bans** allow only election, political, governmental or law-enforcement use. **Commercial-purpose bans** forbid any commercial use, and some define that to include any anticipated monetary gain. Both of these reach a for-profit internal database however tightly it is held. Missouri, Arizona and New Mexico use that "reasonably anticipate monetary gain" definition. It is the clearest form: Missouri's statute bars use "for any purpose in which the purchaser can reasonably anticipate the receipt of monetary gain from the direct or indirect use of the public record" ([RSMo 115.158](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=115.158)). Arizona does the same by cross-reference to § 39-121.03 ([A.R.S. § 39-121.03](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/39/00121-03.htm)).

The secondary compilations get this distinction wrong often enough that verdicts built on them are unreliable. When the verification passes read primary text, three states moved toward *more* access. **Florida** moved from conditional to allowed: the 2004 oath and use limit in § 98.095 is absent from every official compilation from 2005 to 2026 ([Fla. Stat. ch. 98 (2026)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/Chapter98/All)). **Rhode Island** moved from "not allowed" to conditional. Its political-use limit binds only lists the Secretary sells to party chairs and candidates. The public-records route forbids withholding a record "based on the purpose for which the records are sought" ([R.I. Gen. Laws § 38-2-3](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/Statutes/TITLE38/38-2/38-2-3.htm)). **Colorado** moved from "probably" to allowed. The Secretary of State's own FAQ says it releases public voter data "without regard to how these people might use the information," even though the EAC and Justia surveys label Colorado non-commercial ([CO SOS FAQ](https://www.coloradosos.gov/pubs/elections/FAQs/VoterRegistrationData.html)). Verification also overturned an earlier note on **Connecticut**, though without changing its verdict. The restriction came through Public Act 26-1, § 96, not SB 1516, which died on the Senate calendar ([PA 26-1](https://prdext3.cga.ct.gov/2026/ACT/PA/PDF/2026PA-00001-R00SB-00298-PA.PDF)). The pattern runs in one direction. NCSL and EAC labels tend to overstate restrictions, so some of the 23 secondary-source "not allowed" verdicts may turn out to be open.

## Ten jurisdictions impose no use limit, and their files differ sharply in linking value

In the ten open jurisdictions, nothing restricts use. The working question is what each file contains and what it costs. **Florida is the richest.** The Division of Elections issues a monthly statewide extract by mail, and one free request covers every monthly disk for the year. There is no oath or purpose statement. The layout includes **full birth date (MM/DD/YYYY), daytime phone and email**, with voting history in a separate file ([FL DOS extract request](https://dos.fl.gov/elections/data-statistics/voter-registration-statistics/voter-extract-request/); [FL file layout](https://files.floridados.gov/media/708728/final-voter-extract-disk-file-layout-rev-20250516.pdf)). Records exempt under § 119.071 and Address Confidentiality Program participants are already removed. The linking pipeline should not try to re-identify them from property records. **North Carolina** offers a free statewide download, refreshed weekly. G.S. 163-82.10(c) requires county boards to give lists "to any person" and attaches no use clause. The public layout carries **phone number, birth year, age and birth state**. DOB, SSN, driver's license number and email are confidential ([G.S. 163-82.10](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_163/GS_163-82.10.html); [NCSBE layout](https://s3.amazonaws.com/dl.ncsbe.gov/data/layout_ncvoter.txt)).

**Ohio** makes its statewide database a public record under ORC 149.43 and publishes a free download. As amended by SB 293 (effective March 20, 2026), ORC 3503.13(A)(2) withholds SSN, driver's license or ID number, **telephone and email**, ACP records and redacted public-service-worker addresses ([ORC 3503.13](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3503.13)). A third-party tutorial shows a DATE_OF_BIRTH column in the county files ([r4campaigns](https://r4campaigns.netlify.com/state-voter-files)). DOB is not on the exemption list, but the official layout could not be checked: the download page was under maintenance on November 25, 2026. **Michigan** releases the Qualified Voter File to anyone on a FOIA request. The form has no use certification, and MCL 168.509gg exempts fields without restricting how recipients use the data. Each list carries name, residential and mailing address, **year of birth**, registration date, status, voter ID and history. Phone, email and month and day of birth are withheld ([MDOS QVF form](https://www.michigan.gov/-/media/Project/Websites/sos/02lehman/FOIA_FORM.pdf); [MCL 168.509gg](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-168-509gg)). **Colorado** sells a $50 statewide report or a $1,000 election-cycle subscription updated weekly. Its public fields are name, residential address, party, phone and gender identity where the voter provided them, **birth year**, and voting history ([CO SOS form](https://www.coloradosos.gov/pubs/elections/forms/dataRequests.pdf)).

The remaining five open jurisdictions are weaker for linking. **Wisconsin** sells the full WisVote list through Badger Voters for $25 plus $5 per thousand records, capped at **$12,500**. The Elections Commission says "Wisconsin law does not place any restrictions on the use of data obtained through this website." It never releases date of birth, age or age range, but it does include contact information the voter supplied ([Badger Voters FAQ](https://badgervoters.wi.gov/faq)). **Mississippi** gives the statewide file free each week, and under the Public Records Act at no more than actual cost. § 23-15-165(6) strips **SSN, phone, email, DOB and age**, so matching must rest on name and address alone ([MS SoS weekly program](https://www.sos.ms.gov/content/documents/about_us/2025%20Voter%20File%20Weekly%20Distribution%20Program.pdf)). **Alaska** lets "any person" buy the list for a fee the director sets. AS 15.07.195 makes age, DOB, voter ID and birthplace confidential ([AS 15.07.127](https://www.akleg.gov/basis/statutes.asp?media=print&secStart=15.07.127&secEnd=15.07.127)). It also has a narrow debt-specific channel: confidential fields "may be released ... to a person holding a writ of execution against the person or property of the voter" ([AS 15.07.195](https://www.akleg.gov/basis/statutes.asp?media=print&secStart=15.07.195&secEnd=15.07.195)). **The District of Columbia** provides the entire list free by secure link. The request form has no purpose field, and the D.C. Code has no use clause. The fields beyond name, registration data and optional voter history were not confirmed ([DCBOE request form](https://www.dcboe.org/getmedia/6e2e6a71-294c-43a5-8bb1-02c151372f7b/Data-Request-Form_2023.pdf); [D.C. Code § 1-1001.07](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-1001.07)). **Arkansas** is the weakest-sourced open verdict. Search snippets of the Secretary of State's form say "there are currently no state laws restricting use of the data that the SOS releases." The form URL now returns a 404, and the statute itself was never read ([AR SoS FAQ](https://www.sos.arkansas.gov/frequently-asked-questions/35)). Confirm the current Arkansas form before buying.

## Six states open the file only if voter data never drives outbound contact

The six conditional states share one design rule. **Voter-derived names and addresses must never feed solicitation, mailing, advertising or transfer.** Several also screen who may request the file. **Texas** is the most valuable. The Secretary of State "shall furnish information in the statewide computerized voter registration list to any person on request." The only use ban is on use "in connection with advertising or promoting commercial products or services," a **Class A misdemeanor** ([Tex. Elec. Code § 18.066](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._election_code_section_18.066); [§ 18.067](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._election_code_section_18.067)). The current form 24-1 (rev. 10/2026) requires a notarized affidavit that tracks the statute word for word. It charges tiered fees starting at $93.75 plus $0.0005 per voter, and its record layout includes **full DOB** ([SOS form 24-1](https://www.sos.texas.gov/elections/forms/pol-sub/24-1f.pdf)). **Idaho** lets "any person" buy at reproduction cost. It bars only using the list "for the purpose of mailing or delivering any advertisement or offer ... or ... any solicitation for money, services, or anything of value" ([Idaho Code § 34-437A](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/Title34/T34CH4/SECT34-437A/)). Willful violation falls under the default election-offense penalty: a fine of up to **$1,000, up to five years in prison, or both** ([Idaho Code § 18-2315](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/Title18/T18CH23/SECT18-2315/)). The phrase "solicitation for money" almost certainly covers collection letters. In Idaho, voter-derived addresses must be flagged and kept out of every mail workflow.

**South Carolina** has a solicitation-only ban but a buyer gate. § 30-2-50 bars obtaining or using public-record personal information "for commercial solicitation," punishable by up to **$500, one year in jail, or both**. § 30-2-30(3) defines that term narrowly, as phone, mail or email contact "for the purpose of selling or marketing a consumer product or service" ([S.C. Code Title 30, ch. 2](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t30c002.php)). The obstacle is the buyer. The Election Commission sells "to a qualified elector" and states "You must be a registered South Carolina voter to purchase a list" ([SC SEC](https://scvotes.gov/resources/sale-of-voter-registration-lists/)). A company would need an SC-registered individual to buy, and the SEC could treat a purchase made for a corporate principal as outside § 7-3-20(D)(13). The file includes **full DOB** and participation in the last two statewide cycles. **New Jersey** carries a solicitation ban that the earlier notes missed. Under N.J.S.A. 19:31-18.1(c), no person may use the lists "as a basis for commercial or charitable solicitation," and a violator is a disorderly person fined up to **$500**. Lists go to "any voter," county electronic copies are capped at **$375**, and the addresses of Daniel's Law covered persons are omitted at the source ([P.L. 2021, c. 371](https://pub.njleg.gov/bills/2020/PL21/371_.PDF)). A company may also request the file under OPRA. That route requires certifying whether the use is commercial ([OPRA as amended 2024](https://nj.gov/grc/laws/act/Readable%20Version%20of%20OPRA%20%28Sept%202024%29%28P.L.%202024,%20c.16%29.pdf)). The design risk is re-linking a covered person's address from property records and then disclosing it.

**Rhode Island** is open only through the right door. A request under the Access to Public Records Act and §§ 17-9.1-15 and 17-9.1-21 carries no statutory use limit, and § 17-9.1-21 expressly preserves the public's right to "the same information under the access to Public Records Acts" ([§ 17-9.1-21](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/Statutes/TITLE17/17-9.1/17-9.1-21.htm)). The § 17-6-5 political-use restriction attaches only to lists sold to party chairs and candidates "and to no one else" ([§ 17-6-5](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/Statutes/TITLE17/17-6/17-6-5.htm)). In August 2024 the Secretary of State restored **full dates of birth** to the file ([Providence Journal via AOL](https://aol.com/full-transparency-restored-ri-secretary-085927992.html)). The request form itself was not reviewed, so confirm that the office has not added a use statement by policy. **Indiana** is closed statewide. Its county route opens only where the county election board's uniform policy permits copies, which IC 3-7-27-6(c) authorizes "notwithstanding any other statute." The requester signs State Form VRG-24, promising not to use the data to solicit "merchandise, goods, services, or subscriptions" and not to "sell, loan, give away or otherwise deliver" it for non-political purposes. Using voter data to solicit is a **Class B infraction**, rising to a Class A misdemeanor for a repeat knowing violation ([VRG-24](https://forms.in.gov/download.aspx?id=11404)). The Public Access Counselor has said the state-level redactions of DOB and voting history bind only the Election Division. County extracts can carry both ([PAC 17-INF-15](https://secure.in.gov/pac/files/informal/17-INF-15.pdf)). Some boards refuse wholesale lists, so expect county-by-county outcomes ([2026 IN Voter Registration Guidebook](https://www.in.gov/sos/elections/files/2026-Voter-Registration-Guidebook-Revised-Apr-2026.FINAL.pdf)).

The gray zone across Texas, New Jersey and South Carolina is debt-collection contact. Contacting someone about an existing debt is not obviously "advertising," "selling or marketing a consumer product," or "commercial solicitation." Offering debt relief, refinancing or a property purchase plainly is. The defensible posture is an internal-only identity-resolution database, with a documented purpose and voter-derived fields segregated from every outreach system.

## Thirty-five states close the file, and some ban continued use of data already held

The closed states reach the same result by different routes, and the route determines the exposure. **Access gates** bar a private company before use becomes an issue. Massachusetts declares the central registry "not a matter of public record" ([G.L. c. 51 § 47C](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleVIII/Chapter51/Section47C)). Illinois limits electronic data to SBE-registered political committees and government entities. Business use is a **Class 4 felony**, and the Attorney General went to court in 2024 against a publisher that was "not a political committee and could not have legally obtained the voter information" ([10 ILCS 5/4-8](https://ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/001000050K4-8.htm); [Capitol Fax](https://capitolfax.com/2024/05/08/ag-raoul-asks-court-to-force-timponeproft-papers-to-remove-personal-voter-information-from-its-websites)). North Dakota, Virginia, Wyoming and Indiana's statewide channel give lists only to candidates, parties and committees. Minnesota and Maryland sell only to their own registered voters. Since November 1, 2025, Oklahoma limits access to Oklahoma residents and citizens, candidates and others authorized by law ([SB 1086](https://www.oklegislature.gov/cf_pdf/2025-26%20ENGR/SB/SB1086%20ENGR.PDF)).

**Purpose tests** do the work in Pennsylvania, New York, Minnesota, Nebraska, Utah, New Mexico, Tennessee, California, Hawaii, Iowa, Maine and Delaware. Minnesota's text is typical: no one may use the list "for purposes unrelated to elections, political activities, or law enforcement" ([Minn. Stat. 201.091](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/201.091)). Utah rewrote its regime in 2026. It now allows use only for "a political purpose" or another expressly authorized one, makes violation a **class A misdemeanor**, and releases an age range in place of DOB ([Utah Code § 20A-2-607](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title20A/Chapter2/C20A-2-S607_2026031820260318.html)). **Commercial-purpose bans** govern Georgia, Kansas, Missouri, South Dakota, Oregon, Montana, West Virginia, Kentucky, Arizona, Alabama and Connecticut. Kentucky's regulation defines commercial use to include use "for profit" ([31 KAR 3:010](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/kar/downloads/docs/16761/document.engrossed.pdf)). South Dakota pairs a Class 1 misdemeanor with a civil penalty of up to **$2,000 per violation** ([SDCL 12-4-41](https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Statutes/12-4-41.html)). Arizona makes any non-election use a **class 6 felony**. § 16-168 is unchanged: HB2425 stalled in the Senate, and the Governor vetoed SB1375 in 2025 and SB1040 in 2026 ([A.R.S. § 16-168](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/16/00168.htm)).

Washington shows why the regulation, not only the statute, matters. Its felony (up to five years or $10,000, plus $5 per item mailed) reaches only lists used "for the purpose of mailing or delivering" advertisements or solicitations, so an internal database escapes the statute. But the Secretary of State's rule says flatly that "such voter registration lists may not be used for commercial purposes" ([WAC 434-324-130](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/law/wac/WAC%20434%20%20TITLE/WAC%20434%20-324%20%20CHAPTER/WAC%20434%20-324%20-130.htm); [RCW 29A.08.740](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/Law/RCW/RCW%20%2029A%20TITLE/RCW%20%2029A.%2008%20%20CHAPTER/RCW%20%2029A.%2008%20.740.htm)). Nevada runs the other way. The statute is narrow: NRS 293.440(6) binds only party committees that receive free lists. But the Secretary of State's 2026 Elections Procedures Manual applies an election-only, no-sale rule to every recipient and verifies each applicant's "qualification" ([NRS 293.440](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-293.html#NRS293Sec440); [NV 2026 Procedures Manual](https://epubs.nsla.nv.gov/statepubs/epubs/779609-2026.pdf)). A paid county purchase under 293.440(1) has a textual argument, but it is a question for counsel, not a go.

Two exposures in the closed states go beyond the use ban itself. **Required affidavits create independent liability.** Signing Pennsylvania's statement (unsworn falsification), Vermont's sworn affirmation (perjury), Nebraska's oath (election falsification, a **Class IV felony**), Virginia's § 24.2-407 statement (felony false statement) or Wyoming's perjury affirmation while intending a locate use is a separate offense ([Neb. Rev. Stat. 32-330](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=32-330&print=true); [Va. Code § 24.2-407](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/24.2-407/)). **New bans cover data already in hand.** Connecticut now bars "personal, private or commercial" use and even "display in any other format," with SEEC civil penalties of up to $2,000 per offense, effective March 3, 2026 ([OLR 2026-R-0082](https://prdext2.cga.ct.gov/2026/rpt/pdf/2026-R-0082.pdf)). Alabama's Act 2026-570, effective June 1, 2026, makes it unlawful to "use voter data ... for commercial purposes." Its only carve-out is for Fair Campaign Practices Act electioneering, and it states **no penalty clause**, so enforcement theory is a question for counsel ([HB67 enrolled](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2026RS/HB67-enr.pdf)). Both statutes regulate use, not only acquisition. Any Connecticut or Alabama voter records the company bought earlier must be purged or quarantined.

## Substitutes in closed states are narrower, staler or license-bound

No single public source replaces a statewide voter file for finding non-owners and renters at a current address. Each substitute is narrower in coverage, older in its addresses, or governed by its own use restrictions. The documented options sort into five groups. Three other groups look useful but are legally off-limits for this purpose.

**Court records are the strongest substitute for this use case**, because they already tie people to debts. Several closed states run formal bulk programs. Maryland sells District Court civil judgment data on subscription, at **$2,775** for twelve years of judgments plus the first monthly update ([MD Courts DCA-107](https://mdcourts.gov/sites/default/files/court-forms/district/forms/acct/dca107.pdf/dca107.pdf)). Minnesota publishes a bulk-extract program, and Connecticut gives standardized bulk civil data to authorized third parties ([MN Courts FAQ](https://MNCourts.gov/mncourtsgov/media/Research-Evaluation/FAQ-Bulk-Data-Extracts.pdf); [Pew](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/fact-sheets/2023/09/how-to-share-civil-justice-data-with-third-parties-to-improve-public-knowledge-over)). These are contracts, and they bind later sealing and expungement. Eviction data is shrinking. California masks unlawful-detainer filings for 60 days unless the landlord wins at trial in that window ([CCP § 1161.2](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2019/code-ccp/part-3/title-3/chapter-4/section-1161-2/)). South Carolina's 2026 Act No. 214 will remove eviction records from public indexes seven years after disposition, starting January 1, 2027 ([S.C. Code Title 30, ch. 2](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t30c002.php)). **Unclaimed-property files** add breadth in two of the largest closed states. California's Controller offers CSV downloads, and New York's Comptroller delivers an owner-name file with last-known addresses by secure FTP. New York also regulates the "location service providers" that use that file to contact owners ([NY OSC](https://www.osc.ny.gov/unclaimed-funds/resources/location-service-providers)). These addresses come from holders and are often years old. They work better as historical address links than as current locators.

The commercial "current address" products carry the tightest strings. **USPS NCOALink** is licensed only to update a list owner's own mailing list "used to prepare that Entity's Deliverables for delivery by the USPS," and the list owner must sign a Processing Acknowledgement Form ([USPS End User License](https://postalpro.usps.com/mnt/glusterfs/2020-01/End_User_License.pdf); [PAF Guide](https://postalpro.usps.com/mnt/glusterfs/2018-12/PAF_Guide.pdf)). A no-mailing database cannot use it as a lookup. NCOALink fits only if the company actually mails notices to people it already holds. **Credit-header data** is sold under GLBA exceptions. The FTC's 2001 Trans Union opinion treated it as distinct from consumer reports. Consumer advocates still contest that status, and the CFPB's December 2024 proposal to treat such sellers as consumer reporting agencies was withdrawn on May 15, 2025, leaving the question unsettled ([FTC Trans Union opinion](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/cases/010508transunionmemoopinion.pdf); [90 FR, May 15, 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-05-15/html/2025-08644.htm)). **Motor-vehicle records** are governed by the DPPA (18 U.S.C. § 2721). The notes list permissible uses that include verifying information and recovering on a debt, but they drew those from background knowledge, not the statute text. Read § 2721(b) before relying on them ([18 U.S.C. § 2721](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/USCODE-2023-title18/USCODE-2023-title18-partI-chap123-sec2721)). Several apparent substitutes are unusable. FEC contributor data may not be used "for commercial purposes" ([FEC](https://www.fec.gov/updates/sale-or-use-contributor-information)). Florida and North Carolina keep concealed-carry holder lists confidential ([Fla. Stat. § 790.0601](https://flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2021/790.0601)). California adds fines of **$10,000 to $50,000** when registry information is used to commit a misdemeanor, and it generally bars registry use in credit and housing decisions ([CA Megan's Law](https://meganslaw.ca.gov/AboutMegansLaw/PenaltiesForMisuse)).

Two overlays apply whatever the source. The first is the **FCRA**. A compilation becomes a consumer report when it is furnished to others or used for eligibility decisions, and disclaimers do not cure that. Spokeo paid $800,000, and TruthFinder and Instant Checkmate paid **$5.8 million** ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/09/ftc-says-truthfinder-instant-checkmate-deceived-users-about-background-report-accuracy-violated-fcra); [PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/465241/spokeo_data_broker_to_pay_800000_in_ftc_privacy_case.html)). An internal database that is never furnished to third parties sits on safer ground, but that view rests on the statutory definition, not on a ruling in this record. The second overlay is **data-broker registration**. California, Vermont, Oregon and Texas require it, and Connecticut and New Jersey reportedly added registries in 2026. These laws mainly target sellers, but Texas's 2025 amendments may reach businesses that merely process third-party data ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/data-broker-rules-from-regulators-may-catch-businesses-off-guard); [DataGrail](https://www.datagrail.io/blog/data-privacy/data-broker-compliance/)). In closed states, **general public-records "commercial purpose" rules** can also follow the substitutes. Arizona § 39-121.03 imposes treble-fee exposure for undisclosed commercial use of any public record ([A.R.S. § 39-121.03](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/39/00121-03.htm)). Washington's RCW 42.56.070(8) bars agencies from releasing any list of individuals for commercial purposes unless a law specifically authorizes it, and a Washington AG opinion reads the bar as "not limited to situations in which individuals are directly contacted" ([WA AGO](https://atg.wa.gov/ago-opinions/authority-public-agencies-allow-inspection-and-copying-lists-individuals)). In Washington, agency license lists face the same commercial-lists bar as the voter file.

## Conclusion

The workable national design is a voter-file layer for the 16 open jurisdictions, paired with a court-and-property layer everywhere else. It needs three hard controls built into the data model, not left to policy. Every voter-derived field carries a state-of-origin tag. Those fields are barred from any outreach, mailing or export path, which satisfies the act-based bans in Texas, Idaho, South Carolina, New Jersey and Indiana. Connecticut and Alabama records are purged now, because those bans reach data already held. The requester gates in New Jersey, South Carolina and Indiana are the real operational bottleneck. A company may need a registered in-state individual or county-by-county approvals, and the agencies have not said whether a purchase made for a corporate principal qualifies.

The verification pass also changes how to treat the rest of the map. Secondary sources consistently overstated restrictions, and primary text opened Florida, Rhode Island and Colorado. The cheapest way to expand coverage is targeted primary-text checks of West Virginia, New Hampshire, Louisiana, New York's county lists and Nevada's county route, not new data purchases. The policy trend runs against this use case: Connecticut, Alabama, Oklahoma and Utah narrowed access or fields in 2025–2026, and no state in this record reopened a closed file by statute. Treat today's open list as a ceiling. Recheck each state's statute and request form immediately before each acquisition.
