# Free files fill most gaps; judgments stay locked

The repo has fully closed only 2 of the 51 target items: an adapter for every source, and CI tests on every adapter. Another 17 are partial and 32 are missing. Most of the missing data can be filled for free. **County ArcGIS and Socrata layers** cover tax-deed, tax-lien, sheriff-sale and land-bank listings that the auction platforms block. **Six public parcel layers** would add owner names for roughly 8–9 million parcels. **SBA 7(a)/504 FOIA files** give named borrowers with charge-off dates and amounts. **HUD's FHA REO FeatureServer** is national and needs no login. The **FJC bankruptcy IDB** carries the Schedule A real-property total for every case since FY2008, and **CourtListener's free bulk dockets** supply the debtor names that the IDB lacks. One gap does not close for free: **private recorded judgments** (Texas abstracts, Ohio certificates of judgment, Pennsylvania prothonotary indexes, Florida clerks other than the three loaded, and Georgia's paid GSCCCA index) still require records requests or payment. The bigger risk is software, not data. The hosted loader builds each source only once. Nothing snapshots sale listings day to day. Health checks track no row counts. `config/sol.yaml` and `config/rules.yaml` are entirely null. Three of the 11 registered court RSS feeds now fail, including both West Virginia feeds for the pilot state. So the first builds are a recurring snapshot runner with drift alerts, the lien-rule table, and the cheap owner-name and sale-layer configs. Several sources are legally off-limits or need counsel first: FEC contributor data is barred from commercial use, ProPublica's 990 terms forbid resale, CourtListener's licence is unresolved, PACER terms bar fee-avoiding harvesting, and Tennessee, Montana and Indiana attach use conditions to parcel owner names.

Two patterns stand out. First, every commercial auction platform (RealAuction, LienHub, GovEase, Bid4Assets, SRI/Zeus, EnergyNet, HUD Home Store) is unusable, so a sale aggregator has to come from the governments that publish the lists, not from the platforms that run the auctions. Second, terms of use were not read for any of the county ArcGIS layers recommended below. Most carry no explicit licence. A few do, such as Fulton County's CC-BY-4.0 and Seattle's Public Domain. The repo's `no_resale` and `attribution_required` flags need a per-source review before anything is redistributed.

## County GIS layers rebuild the sale aggregator the platforms block

The repo has eight sale-adjacent county sources, all lien-style ArcGIS or WPRDC configs under `config/liens/`. It has no federal sale feed, no land-bank or state-surplus source, no personal-property or mineral-lease source, and no sale-results feed. The research points to three workable channels. The first is current county layers published by treasurers, auditors and sheriffs. The second is a few federal machine-readable feeds: HUD REO, the GSA Auctions API, and the **Federal Register API**, which returned **1,594 "notice of realty action" documents** (newest 2026-11-19) and **1,234 HUD Title V surplus-property notices** ([FR API](https://www.federalregister.gov/api/v1/documents.json?conditions%5Bterm%5D=%22notice%20of%20realty%20action%22&per_page=3&order=newest)). The third is the ArcGIS Hub and Socrata discovery APIs themselves, the only realistic route to 50-state county coverage. These layers are point-in-time. Douglas County CO's lien layer returned **0 rows** two days after an edit, so adapters must snapshot every run, or the history is lost.

The sale layers fit the repo's existing ArcGIS lien adapter with a `parcel:` join key, so most of this table is configuration work, not code. The two highest-value additions, Milwaukee and Dakota, carry **outcomes** (price and purchaser), and Milwaukee already has a match run. HUD REO and land-bank ownership also serve as **negative screens** for the equity graph: a parcel the debtor already lost to HUD, a land bank or a tax deed carries no reachable equity. The one generic new adapter worth writing is a Federal Register client. Turning its PLSS legal descriptions into parcels takes a deterministic parser, which is real work, so treat the client as a notice feed first.

The parcel fill is the highest-leverage data item for the judgment-to-equity graph, because every other source joins through owner names. By the research's sum of live row counts, Maricopa, Tennessee's 86 counties, SanGIS, Montana, Clark NV, King WA and the 0.2–0.35M-parcel counties together add owner names for about 8–9 million parcels. In the states with no owner names, the **latest grantee on a deed-sales file** is the best owner proxy: King County's sales file, NJ SR-1A, the MD SDAT grantor on the next sale, and Norfolk's grantee. The same files supply the sale prices that the transfer detector and the overassessment finder both need.

Two court findings change the plan. The first is a **free join that gives "named debtor plus scheduled real-property value"**. FJC's CASEKEY (district, office, docket) should match CourtListener's `docket_number_core`, attaching `REALPROP`, `SECURED`, county and discharge status to a named debtor before any PACER spend. A bankruptcy flag is a cheap negative filter, because the automatic stay blocks enforcing a judgment. It also tells which liens probably survived discharge. The second is a **data requirement that varies by state**, set by how each state creates a judgment lien. In docket states such as PA, NJ, NY, NC and WI, a docketed court judgment is itself the lien in that county, so a court feed is enough. In recording states such as CA, TX, FL, AZ, IL, MI and OH, a judgment shows only that a lien could exist. Proving it takes a recorder hit. Massachusetts, New Hampshire and Rhode Island require a levy. That classification comes from the research's statute table, which marks the creation column as unverified. Adding `lien_created_by` and `homestead_cap` columns to `sol.yaml` would let the rules decide which states need recorder data. Texas and Florida homestead immunity makes owner occupancy a critical flag in those states ([NCLC Table 2](https://www.nclc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/2025.12_Report_No-Fresh-Start_Table-02.pdf)).

The RSS gap needs a fix now. Five full bankruptcy feeds in the buyer's states are not enabled: `ohsb, pamb, pawb, lamb, lawb`. Fourteen more full feeds are also off, including `ilnb, mieb, mdb, caeb, canb, casb` and `insb`. Courts whose feeds are off or failing (`nceb, nyeb, miwb, txsb, flsb, alnb`) can be backfilled only from CourtListener's quarterly bulk. RSS covers only about the last 24 hours, so every day without polling is history lost for good.

## Conclusion

The gap analysis shifts the bottleneck. Two earlier rounds concluded that free named judgments barely exist outside a few counties, and this round confirms it. But it also shows that almost everything around the judgments is free: owners, sale lists, bankruptcy status, entity officers, federal loan defaults and aircraft. So the graph's ceiling is set less by data access than by two internal gaps. One is a pipeline that never re-fetches, so it cannot see change. The other is a rules layer whose every parameter is null, so it cannot decide anything. Fix those two and each free source above compounds: snapshots turn listings into results and redemptions, owner fills turn court names into parcels, and the bankruptcy and transfer flags turn matches into buy-or-skip calls.

The sale aggregator and the judgment graph share more infrastructure than it first appears. A tax-deed list, a sheriff-sale result, a HUD REO record and a surplus-funds row are each an event on a parcel. Each one either destroys a debtor's equity or converts it to cash that a judgment creditor can claim. Built on one snapshot table keyed to parcels, the aggregator becomes the graph's best negative screen and its most direct path to cash. For private judgment inventory, a disciplined records-request programme will add more than any further open-data search.
