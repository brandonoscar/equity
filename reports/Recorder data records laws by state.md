# Ranking 51 jurisdictions for free recorder data

Florida, New York, Texas, Georgia and Maryland are the most likely places to get a free or near-free, machine-readable recorder index (grantor, grantee, document type, date, book/page, instrument number) through an honest public records request. Ten jurisdictions are close to dead ends for this business: Washington, Arizona, Maine, Alabama, Arkansas, Tennessee, Delaware, Kentucky, Virginia and Indiana. Three things separate the top from the bottom. The first is whether a recorder-specific fee statute overrides the general records act's cost cap, and most of these statutes price by the page or the instrument, which is ruinous at bulk scale. The second is whether the state treats an export from an existing database as an existing record or as a new record the office need not create. The third is whether an honest statement that the requester is a commercial judgment buyer triggers a surcharge, a bar, or a residency refusal. Six states (AL, AR, DE, KY, TN, VA) let a non-resident's request be refused outright. Washington bars giving lists of individuals to commercial requesters. Arizona has required every requester since 2025 to state any commercial purpose, under threat of treble damages. These findings are **provisional**. In all six regional research passes, official legislature and agency sites were unreachable, so most statute text was read only as search-result excerpts and some was supplied from the researchers' background knowledge. A lawyer should review the specific statutes before any request is sent. On the evidence available, a 10-state pilot of automated requests is still a sound bet if the requests ask only for index fields, from an existing export function, with an itemized estimate before any work.

## One rubric replaces six regional scoring habits

The six regional teams each scored states 1–5, but they weighed different things, so their raw scores cannot be compared. The Mid-Atlantic/Southeast note scored mostly on the face of the statute and gave West Virginia a 4 without any evidence from practice. The Plains/Mountain note capped every state at 3 and gave Colorado a 1 because counties already sell bulk feeds. The West note scored commercial-purpose bars most harshly. The South Central note scored explicitly for a non-resident requester. The Northeast note marked down Vermont, Connecticut and Rhode Island partly because the work is spread across hundreds of town clerks. That is a logistics problem, not a legal one. The Midwest note was the only one to define its endpoints. Left unadjusted, these habits would rank states by which researcher covered them.

The normalized score uses one additive rubric. Every jurisdiction starts at **3**, then:

| Factor | +1 | 0 | −1 / −2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **F: general fee rule for an electronic copy** | Duplication or media cost only, no search fee, or a meaningful free-time allowance (for example 2–4 free staff hours) | Cost-based with billable staff time or programming | — |
| **R: recorder-specific override** | — | None found, or one that sends electronic copies back to the general act | −1: a per-page, per-instrument, county-set, database-sale or paid-bulk statute likely controls the price of an electronic index. −2: bulk is available only by the office's voluntary agreement |
| **E: database extracts** | Statute, appellate case or AG opinion that directly treats an export through existing programs as owed (or requires a cost estimate rather than a refusal) | Format-only rules, or no authority | −1: extracts or customizing expressly discretionary or excused |
| **C: honest commercial disclosure** | — | No consequence, or certification and longer timing only | −1: a commercial-only fee, a commercial-value add-on or a markup |

Three caps apply after the factors are added. **Blockers set the score to 1.** These are an honest request that can be refused outright: residency-only states for a non-resident (AL, AR, DE, KY, TN, VA), Washington's commercial list bar, and Maine's voluntary-only bulk rule. Each blocked state also gets a resident score. An ambiguous residency rule (NH) or an optional non-resident or automated-request surcharge (IN) is −1 rather than a blocker. **A 5 requires confirmed free bulk recorder data somewhere in the state.** Without it, a strong statutory score is capped at 4. Only Florida (Hillsborough's daily index files) and New York (NYC's ACRIS, which the Northeast note reports this project already ingests) qualify. **Background-only items do not move a score in either direction.** A factor needs a cited finding. That is why Massachusetts stays at 3 although its reported fee rules sound favorable: its 2016 reform terms appear only as background. **The number of custodians is excluded from the score.** It is used only to break ties within a tier, along with how much of the state's case rests on cited rather than background authority and how much legal risk the state poses to a judgment buyer.

Nineteen jurisdictions moved. The largest rises were Wyoming (2→4), because § 16-4-202(d) excuses extracting data only if it would impair the agency's duties ([RCFP WY](https://www.rcfp.org/open-government-guide/wyoming/)), and Maryland, Idaho, Nevada, Iowa and Connecticut (3→4), because cited free-hour or extract rules were under-credited. Colorado rose from 1 to 2, the same score as other paid-bulk states such as Pennsylvania. Ohio fell from 4 to 3 because its bulk-commercial pricing applies to an honest judgment buyer. Delaware, Virginia and Kentucky fell to 1 under the blocker rule. Illinois and Oklahoma fell to 2 for negotiated-bulk and commercial-search-fee exposure. Alaska fell from 3 to 2 because its recorder product fees are set by regulation and not waived. Wyoming's jump rests on a single RCFP summary, and it should be read as "3 until the text is confirmed."

The tiers fit together. The top dozen combine a cost-only or free-hours fee rule with either an extract duty or the absence of any recorder override. **No top-tier state has a commercial surcharge that an honest judgment buyer would trigger.** The middle tier is mostly cost-based states where an export is plausible but staff time is billable. The 2s are almost all recorder-override states, where the general act says "cost" but a recorder statute, regulation or ordinance says "per page," "per instrument," or "by contract." The 1s are the states where the request itself can be refused, or where honest disclosure triggers commercial pricing plus paid bulk programs.

The pilot should be the top ten ranked jurisdictions. Each one offers a different statutory hook, so the pilot also tests which legal theory clerks actually honor. The hooks are New York's burden-on-agency rule, Georgia's existing-program duty, Texas's statutory routing of electronic real-property copies to cost, Maryland's searchable-format rule, the free-hours rules in West Virginia and Idaho, Iowa's IPIB opinion, and Connecticut's FIC signal against per-page fees. One or two offices per state is enough. The goal is to calibrate the ranking, not to cover each state.

## Templates should request index fields from an existing export, estimate first

Across all 51 jurisdictions, three things decide whether a request gets a cheap CSV or a four-figure quote. The first is **asking for index fields only**: grantor, grantee, document type, recording date, book/page, instrument number and, where indexed, consideration and parcel ID, with no images. Most recorder fee statutes are written per page or per copy of a recorded document. Examples are Pennsylvania's $0.50/page, Wisconsin's $2/$1 for electronic copies ([FindLaw 59.43](https://codes.findlaw.com/wi/municipalities-ch-59-to-68/wi-st-59-43/)), South Dakota's per-copy register fee, and Maine's per-image bulk schedule. A pure index file is the strongest ground for arguing those fees do not apply. Dropping mailing-address fields also weakens New York's name-and-address list exemption, Washington's lists-of-individuals reasoning, Idaho's mailing-list bar and Kansas's names-and-addresses rule.

The second is **framing the request as a copy of an existing record**. The Mid-Atlantic note's suggested wording works across states: "an export of existing index tables (grantor, grantee, doc type, recording date, book/page, instrument number, consideration) using your system's existing export/report function, in CSV or the native format you keep it in." It tracks Georgia's existing-programs statute, Maryland's § 4-205, Virginia's medium rule, Florida's *Seigle* test, Utah's "without reformatting or conversion" test and Iowa's IPIB opinion. Where an office already sells a daily feed or subscriber file (Colorado, Utah, Nevada, Arizona, Tarrant County), ask for a copy of that file for a short date range. That makes "no duty to create" irrelevant and turns the dispute into one about price. Asking for a narrow window first, such as the last 30 days, also tests per-instrument pricing cheaply. Under North Dakota's cap of $0.50 per instrument, 100,000 instruments could cost up to $50,000 ([11-18-05](https://codes.findlaw.com/nd/title-11-counties/nd-cent-code-sect-11-18-05/)).

The third is **requiring an itemized estimate before any work**. Missouri lets a requester demand an estimate ([610.026](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=610.026)). Nebraska gives 10 business days to accept, narrow or withdraw after one. Oregon requires notice above $25, and Texas requires a written estimate for programming. Several states allow prepayment demands (Pennsylvania, Georgia above $500, Arkansas above $25, Connecticut at $10 or more). Every template should therefore say: "Please do not incur any fee without my written approval. If any fee applies, send an itemized estimate listing staff hours, rates, programming and media, and the statute authorizing each charge." That keeps the pilot inside its budget of free unless the owner approves a fee, and the estimates become the dataset for recalibrating the ranking.

Honest wording is a legal requirement as well as a policy. Requests should go out under a real person's name and address. They should state commercial use wherever a state asks, which includes New Jersey, Illinois, Arizona, Kentucky and Ohio's bulk-commercial assurance. They should never claim a residency the sender lacks. Automation needs one caution: Indiana's 2026 amendments let agencies delay and surcharge requests from automated systems ([PAC](https://secure.in.gov/pac/files/Overview_of_APRA_Changes_under_HEA_1360.pdf)). Automated requests should be identifiable as such and paced, not sent in bursts.

## Judgment buyers face use restrictions that outlast the request

Several states regulate what a requester does with the data after receiving it, not only whether the office releases it. Those rules bite hardest for a business that buys judgments and may contact creditors or debtors. **South Carolina § 30-2-50** makes it a crime to knowingly obtain or use personal information from a public body for commercial solicitation ([Charleston](https://www.charleston-sc.gov/DocumentCenter/View/28078/Foia-Information)). Its definitions and penalties were not read. **Kansas K.S.A. 45-230** bars using names and addresses from public records to sell or offer property or services. Third-party recipients are also bound, and violations carry up to $500 each. Custodians may demand a certification ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/kansas/chapter-45/article-2/section-45-230/); [KDWP FAQ](https://ftp.ksoutdoors.com/content/download/47479/484783/file/FAQs%20Names%20and%20Addresses.pdf)). Offering to buy a judgment arguably "offers a service," so no one should sign a Kansas certification until counsel has decided whether the business's outreach would violate it.

**Washington RCW 42.56.070(8)** bars agencies from providing lists of individuals for commercial purposes. AGO 2019 No. 3 extends this to sortable, queryable databases that can display such lists, treats an alphabetical property-owner list as a list of individuals, and lets the agency ask about purpose ([AGO 2019 No. 3](https://www.atg.wa.gov/ago-opinions/listings-taxable-property-lists-names-individuals-requested-commercial-purpose)). A grantor/grantee index is squarely at risk. **Arizona SB 1372** (Laws 2025, ch. 102) requires every requester to affirm non-commercial use or explain the commercial use, and the fact sheet describes treble damages where a record would not have been provided had the purpose been stated. Under **§ 39-121.03**, a commercial requester must supply a statement and may be charged commercial fees ([House summary](https://www.azleg.gov/legtext/57leg/1R/summary/H.SB1372_051525_SIGNED.DOCX.htm); [FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/az/title-39-public-records-printing-and-notices/az-rev-st-sect-39-121-03/)). An honest Arizona request from this business must state the commercial purpose.

**Illinois** defines commercial purpose as use for sale, resale, or solicitation or advertising of sales or services, and it is a violation to obtain records for a commercial purpose without disclosing it ([IL AG FAQ](https://illinoisattorneygeneral.gov/Page-Attachments/FOIAPAC/FAQ_FOIA_Public.pdf)). **Ohio's** bulk-commercial definition covers surveys, marketing, solicitation and resale, and only a requester who can truthfully assure non-commercial use escapes the surcharge ([R.C. 149.43](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-149.43)). Neither state has an AG opinion on whether internal analytics without resale counts as commercial. The safe course is to disclose. Contract terms add another layer. Indiana bars bulk users from transferring data to third parties for commercial purposes. Wisconsin register contracts may bar resale. Salt Lake County licenses are non-transferable and revocable. New Mexico database sales can carry use restrictions and royalties. Any plan to resell or share derived graph data must be checked against each source's terms.

**The Fair Credit Reporting Act was outside this research, but it needs a flag.** If the land-records graph is ever used to furnish information about individuals to third parties for credit, insurance, employment or similar eligibility decisions, or if the business buys reports for those purposes, FCRA obligations could attach. Debt-collection and judgment-enforcement rules governing contact with debtors were not researched either. Counsel should scope both before outreach begins.

## Conclusion

The map of recorder data access is mainly drawn by **recorder fee statutes, not general records acts**. Nearly every general act promises cost-based access. What separates Texas or Florida from Colorado or Wisconsin is whether the legislature routed electronic land-records copies back to that promise (Texas § 118.011(e), Florida's 1999 cross-reference) or carved them out for per-page, per-instrument or paid-subscription pricing. Bulk recorder data is increasingly treated as a revenue product: Colorado's daily bulk subscriptions, Maine's 2026 voluntary-agreement rule, Pennsylvania's acceptance of market pricing for bulk property data, and Indiana's 2026 surcharge on automated requests. The window for free access through records requests is probably narrowing, which favors starting the pilot soon in the jurisdictions where the law is clearest.

The second lesson is that the business's identity matters as much as its wording. Six states turn away non-residents and two heavily penalize honest commercial disclosure. The resident-variant scores (Virginia 4, Delaware, Tennessee and a Kentucky-registered entity 3) show that a lawful change of requester, such as a resident partner or a foreign-entity registration in Kentucky, could reopen several states, but only with the owner's approval and counsel's sign-off. The pilot's most valuable output will be the itemized estimates. They will show which of the theories in this report clerks actually honor, and they should replace this provisional ranking within one request cycle.
